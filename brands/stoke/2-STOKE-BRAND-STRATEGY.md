# Stoke: Brand Strategy

## Brand Identity

| Element | Decision |
|---------|----------|
| **Brand Name** | Stoke |
| **Category** | Food-swap app for athletes: a new branch diverging from the sports nutrition plan, which tells athletes what to eat, and from the calorie tracker, which counts what they ate. Per the Law of the Category, Stoke does not fight to be second in "sports nutrition coaching," where Fuelin calls itself "the world's first adaptive nutrition coach," or another rung on the calorie ladder MyFitnessPal owns; it creates a ladder it can lead, as Red Bull created "energy drink" instead of fighting Coca-Cola for cola. |
| **Word to Own** | Swap |
| **Verbal Nail** | "Small swaps. Big goals." |
| **Tagline** | Small swaps. Big goals. |

## The Core Idea

Nobody keeps a fire going with one giant log. You keep it alive with small, regular stoking: a log here, a breath of air there, whenever it needs it. That is how amateurs with day jobs reach big goals: not by living by a perfect meal plan, but by making the food they already eat a little better, one swap at a time. Each swap is a stoke; the name, the metaphor and the position are one idea.

## Product

- **What it is:** a food app that makes what you already eat better for your goal, one science-based swap at a time.
- **How it works:** snap a meal (or scan a barcode, or say it). Stoke identifies the food, reads your goal (build muscle, run a marathon, race HYROX) and your training (Strava, Garmin, Apple Health, or sessions you enter), and returns the One Swap: the single change to that meal that does the most for your goal, with one line of why. No meal plan to follow, no daily targets to hit, no food scale. The design leans on what photos read well (what the food is and when you eat it) rather than on gram-precise totals, which photo apps undercount by about a third (NIH/NIDDK, 2026); Stoke asks a single portion question only when the answer would change the swap.
- **The branded artifact: the One Swap**, chosen for each meal by four checks drawn from the sports-nutrition guidelines:

| Check | What It Looks For | Why It Matters |
|-------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Goal fit** | Whether the meal's makeup matches the goal: protein-forward for building muscle, carbohydrate-forward for endurance volume, both for hybrid training | Muscle gains plateau around 1.6 g/kg/day of protein (Morton et al., 2018); endurance training of 1-3 h/day calls for 6-10 g/kg/day of carbohydrate (Thomas, Erdman and Burke, 2016) |
| **Timing** | Whether the food suits its place relative to the next session: fast, low-fibre carbohydrate before; carbohydrate plus protein after; fibre and micronutrients on easy days | The joint position stand advises avoiding "choices high in fat/protein/fibre" before events and periodizing to "the needs of daily training sessions" (Thomas et al., 2016) |
| **Dose** | Whether the amount is in range for this meal: about 0.25-0.4 g/kg of protein, and carbohydrate scaled to the session's length and intensity | ISSN: 0.25 g/kg or 20-40 g of protein per dose (Jäger et al., 2017); 0.4 g/kg per meal across four meals (Schoenfeld and Aragon, 2018) |
| **Quality** | Protein quality and leucine; fibre, iron and calcium when the day allows; processing judged by context, not by rule | 700-3,000 mg of leucine per dose (Jäger et al., 2017); "nutrient-rich carbohydrate sources" for daily fueling (Thomas et al., 2016) |

The checks are ranked differently by goal, and the ranking is published. For muscle building, daily protein adequacy outranks timing, because timing effects disappear once total protein is controlled (Schoenfeld, Aragon and Krieger, 2013); for endurance and HYROX, session carbohydrate and timing come first. Stoke always proposes the change with the biggest effect for the goal, so the swap is small to make but never trivial. The method is reviewed by sports dietitians before launch.

- **The meal score (feedback and play, never a goal):** every meal gets a quick 0-100 read of how well it serves the goal, shown as the ember's glow, with the same one line of why. Its jobs are to teach and to make better choices satisfying. Taking the swap raises the score and stokes the ember; across a week, the ember glows with the meals that served the goal and the swaps taken; and each new kind of swap adds its principle to the user's playbook ("Carbs before long efforts," "Protein at breakfast"), with the study behind it. There are no score targets, no daily quotas and no leaderboards comparing people's food. The evidence sets its rank: game mechanics add a small, real lift to engagement and behavior (Mazeas et al., 2022; Nishi et al., 2024), so the score amplifies the swap and cannot replace it.
- **The Energy Floor (safety by design):** energy is watched as a floor, never budgeted as a ceiling. When logged intake runs low against training load for several days, Stoke says so plainly and suggests adding food; it never praises eating less, and the game never rewards it. This uses the sport's own risk model, energy availability (Mountjoy et al., 2023), with "at risk" language because self-reported intake inflates apparent under-fueling (McHaffie et al., 2025).
- **Convergence flag (Origin of Brands):** Stoke joins a tracker, a recommendation engine and a game, and the risk is being read as three categories glued together ("Cal AI plus a nutrition coach plus a game"), which converged products rarely survive. Mitigation: present one thing, the swap; lead every screen and message with it; keep logging as invisible plumbing and the score as quiet feedback; never market the parts as a bundle.
- **What Stoke leaves out (Law of Sacrifice):** no meal plans, schedules or portion prescriptions; no daily calorie or macro targets; no calorie budget and no "over" warnings; no weight goals and no cut mode; no library of lessons to work through (the principles arrive attached to swaps you actually took); no score leaderboards; no rewards for eating less. Each of these would serve a different word than swap, and the first would make Stoke a smaller Fuelin. In-N-Out's deliberately tiny menu is the model: subtract what the category treats as mandatory.

### The Signature Interaction / Element

**The stoke**: the moment you take a swap. Tap it and the ember draws a breath, brightens and sends up three sparks; the meal's score rises; the one line of why stays on screen. Illustratively: "Tomorrow: 30 km long run. Swap the big salad for a rice bowl with chicken: more carbohydrate for the run, same protein." It happens on the meal screen, in the night-before nudge before key sessions and on the weekly share card ("12 swaps this week"). One swap, one breath of air on the fire: the position in miniature, and the opposite of a plan that tells you what to eat. Offering swaps improved shoppers' choices more than Nutri-Score labels alone in a randomized trial (Jansen et al., 2021) and helped people with less nutrition knowledge most (Schruff-Lim et al., 2024), although swap evidence so far comes from the general population and shows modest effects.

### The User Journey

| Stage | Experience | What the User Feels/Does |
|-------|-----------|--------------------------|
| **1. Snap** | Week one: photograph meals as usual, pick a goal and connect training (or enter sessions). No swaps yet; Stoke learns the week and the user's usual meals. | "This is faster than my calorie app, and nothing is telling me what I can't eat." |
| **2. Swap** | The One Swap appears on the meals that matter most for the goal, with one line of why, plus a night-before nudge before key sessions. | Takes one swap (a rice bowl instead of a salad before the long run) and feels the difference in the session. |
| **3. Stoke** | The ember and the meal score show the effect; the week's glow builds with swaps taken; principles land in the playbook. | Starts making the swap before Stoke suggests it: the habit forms, and the score feels like a game, not a test. |
| **4. Race week** | Stoke looks further ahead with race-week swaps: carbohydrate loading, the race-morning meal, carbohydrate per hour during the race, recovery. | Trusts Stoke with the day that matters, and shares the race-week card. |

The journey carries one abrupt conversion, flagged in the guardrails: people coming from calorie apps or meal plans will look for a budget or a plan, and neither exists by design. Onboarding explains "one swap, not a plan," and energy and macro figures stay available in a detail view for the curious, never as targets.

- Target user: people who train for something (a race time, a heavier lift, a HYROX finish) and want to eat better for it, but won't live by a meal plan or a food scale. Ambitious amateurs with day jobs, work lunches and family dinners, who have been handed dieting tools or plans built for people with time to follow them.

### The Fuelin Difference

Fuelin is the closest competitor, and it shares almost every feature Stoke could ship: photo logging, workout sync, lifting and HYROX goals. The difference is the model, and it is stated the same way every time:

> **"Fuelin tells you what to eat. Stoke makes what you already eat better for your goal, one swap at a time."**

| Difference | Fuelin | Stoke |
|------------|--------|-------|
| **1. Plan vs. swap** | "Builds a daily nutrition plan just for you" that "automatically adjusts to your workouts," and Smart Meals suggests what to cook or order | Starts from the meal you already chose and recommends the one swap that does the most for your goal |
| **2. Targets vs. choices** | "Precise macros and calorie targets," with portion sizing | No daily targets: Stoke rates the choice and shows a better one; the score is feedback, never a quota |
| **3. Lessons vs. learning by doing** | "In-app lessons and videos" on portion control, supplements and energy balance | Each swap carries one line of why, and a light game makes the habit stick: you learn by eating, not by watching |

The rules for using the line are in doc 3, "The Fuelin Difference (Locked)."

### Elevator Pitch

> "If you're training for something big, a marathon, a HYROX, a stronger body, you already know food matters. But your options are a calorie counter built for weight loss, or a nutrition plan that tells you exactly what to eat and when. Most of us have jobs, work lunches and family dinners. Stoke works with the food you already eat: snap your meal and it gives you one swap, backed by sports science, that does the most for your goal. Small swaps. Big goals."

### Competitive Repositioning

**Move 1: Turn the plan's completeness into its weakness (Law of the Opposite).** The leader's strength is precision: Fuelin "tells you exactly what to eat, when to eat it, and why it matters." The weakness inside that strength is that a plan works only if you live by it, and adherence is where diets are won or lost: in a one-year randomized trial of four popular diets in overweight adults, weight loss tracked self-reported adherence (r = 0.60) and not diet type (r = 0.07), and overall adherence was low (Dansinger et al., 2005). Hexis's own partner review makes the point from inside the category, calling logging "the grind" and the product a fit for "structured cyclists training 6+ hours/week" (Roadman Cycling, 2026). Stoke takes the opposite position: start from the life the amateur already has. Pepsi did not out-Coke Coke; it turned the leader's age into a weakness and became "the choice of a new generation." Stoke does not out-plan Fuelin; it makes the plan look like more than an amateur needs.

**Move 2: Demote the calorie to a weight tool (Law of Candor).** Concede what is true: digital self-monitoring was linked to weight loss in 74% of reported occurrences across 39 trials (Patel et al., 2021). Then reposition it: that is the dieter's job, while the athlete's guidelines time nutrition "in relation to sport rather than general daily targets" (Thomas et al., 2016), and many of Stoke's swaps add food, because amateurs under-eat the sessions that matter (only 5.3% of marathoners hit race carbohydrate targets in one 2025 study). MyFitnessPal ("Calorie Counter") and Cal AI are named after the calorie; they cannot tell an athlete to eat more without contradicting their own names. This is the Uncola move: 7-Up became the alternative to cola by defining itself against it.

**Move 3: Replace the lesson with the swap (Law of Sacrifice).** Fuelin teaches through "in-app lessons and videos." The evidence says knowing is not doing: nutrition education raised young endurance athletes' knowledge but "was not enough to change dietary intake," and an app added nothing to learning (Heikkilä et al., 2019); in real stores, education-only interventions did not change purchases while swaps "appeared promising" (Hartmann-Boyce et al., 2018). Stoke gives up lessons and teaches through the meal: one swap, one line of why, and a light game that turns repetition into habit. Duolingo, by its own account the world's most popular way to learn a language, shrank the course to a few minutes of practice a day; Stoke shrinks sports nutrition from a plan and a lecture to one swap per meal.

## Naming

### Name: Stoke

- **Meaning 1**: To stoke a fire is to keep it burning with small, regular attention: a log, a breath of air. Each swap is a stoke.
- **Meaning 2**: "Stoked" means thrilled and fired up: the feeling when a big goal comes within reach.
- **Meaning 3**: "Stoke up" means "to eat one's fill; to eat copious amounts" ("stoke up on pizza," Wiktionary): a name about eating enough, for an audience that tends to eat too little for its training.
- **Tone**: warm, physical, encouraging, a little irreverent. Not clinical, not dieting, not a drill sergeant, not gym-bro.
- **Verb test**: "I stoked my lunch" needs teaching, but the brand gives the verb a home: taking a swap is "the stoke," and "stoke up" already exists as a verb about eating. Natural with use.

### Naming Principles Applied

| Principle | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| No category words in the name | Clean: "stoke" is not food, swap, fuel, track, cal or macro, and it stands apart from the many "Fuel" names in the niche (Fuelin, Exact Fuel, Fuel: AI Nutrition) |
| 1-2 syllables | One |
| Easy to spell and pronounce | One obvious spelling in English; reads cleanly in Spanish and German |
| Evokes the idea, not the function | Evokes tending a fire with small, regular actions, not logging food |
| Multiple reinforcing meanings | Three: tending a fire, fired up, eating one's fill |
| Built-in visual hammer potential | Yes, with a constraint: the drawable image is fire, and a flame commonly stands for "calories burned" in fitness apps, so the hammer is the ember: a fire kept alive by small stokes, not a fire burning calories |

**Caveat:** the name space is crowded and must be cleared before committing. Found on 5 October 2026: App Store apps named "Stoked Metabolic Coaching" (fitness and nutrition coaching), "STOKED Community" (a CrossFit and HYROX gym app), "The Stoke" (studio booking) and "ActiveStoke" (Stoke-on-Trent leisure centres); trainer Kira Stokes markets "The Stoked Method™"; "STOKE" is a pending US trademark filing by Stoke Space Technologies in software and scientific services (serial 98007395); and in the UK the word also means Stoke-on-Trent and Stoke City FC. The domains stoke.app, stokeapp.com, getstoke.com, stoke.fit, stoke.run, getstoke.app, trystoke.com, joinstoke.com, stoke.coach, stokeup.app and stokefuel.com are registered; stokeapp.co was not. Required before launch: trademark clearance in the US, EU and UK (classes 9, 42 and 44, plus 41 if coaching is ever offered), an App Store name check (for example "Stoke: Food Swaps for Athletes"), clearance of the tagline "Small swaps. Big goals." (no App Store name conflict found on 5 October 2026; a trademark search is still required), and a domain plan. If clearance fails, keep the strategy and re-run naming against the same six principles.

## Strategic Guardrails

1. **Never lead with AI or photo logging.** Logging is the mechanism and table stakes: at least eight competitors launched photo logging between November 2024 and May 2026, and Apple gives a version away in iOS 27. Leading with it puts Stoke on Cal AI's ladder, which MyFitnessPal now owns.

2. **Never become a meal plan, and state the Fuelin difference only one way.** No daily plans, meal schedules, portion prescriptions or "what to eat today" screens: the swap is the product. A plan would make Stoke a smaller, later Fuelin and erase the one difference that matters, because nearly every other feature is shared. In every comparison, use the locked line verbatim: "Fuelin tells you what to eat. Stoke makes what you already eat better for your goal, one swap at a time." Public copy that names no competitor uses the public form: "Meal plans tell you what to eat. Stoke makes what you already eat better for your goal, one swap at a time." Never paraphrase the line, and never characterize Fuelin beyond its own published words.

3. **Never make the score the goal.** The meal score is feedback and play: no score targets, no "hit 90 today" streaks, no leaderboards ranking people's food, and never "the food score app" in marketing. If users chase the number instead of the goal, Stoke slides onto the health-score ladder (Yuka, ZOE, Apple), and a game can start rewarding restriction. The score exists to make the swap visible and the habit satisfying, nothing more.

4. **Never call Stoke a calorie counter, diet app or weight-loss tool, and never add weight targets or a cut mode.** A calorie budget makes Stoke a smaller MyFitnessPal; giving up weight goals keeps every swap pointed at training. It also keeps Stoke away from the motive most associated with later disordered behavior among app users: weight management (Hahn et al., 2024). Switchers will ask for the budget back; answer with onboarding that explains the swap and the Energy Floor, never by quietly reintroducing a ceiling.

5. **Never praise eating less, and treat the Energy Floor as launch-blocking.** Before launch, a sports dietitian and an eating-disorder specialist review every message about low intake and every game mechanic; messaging uses "at risk" language and points to professional help. Athletes carry higher eating-disorder rates than non-athletes (13.5% vs. 4.6% among Norwegian elite athletes; Sundgot-Borgen and Torstveit, 2004).

6. **Never moralize food.** No "good," "bad," "clean," "junk" or "cheat." Every swap names its context ("for tomorrow's long run"), because a gel recommended without context reads as an endorsement of junk food, and ultra-processed foods carry real concerns outside sport (Forbes et al., 2026).

7. **Never claim more than the evidence supports.** No "proven to make you faster," no accuracy percentages, no "first," and no invented HYROX science: no published study measures how to fuel HYROX, so Stoke applies the endurance and strength guidelines to a race that is about half running, and funds or partners on real data. Every public number traces to the evidence brief; joint position stands and independent trials beat industry-funded studies, several of which carry dairy, beef, supplement or gel-maker ties. The most quotable failure in this category is an accuracy claim: Cal AI's "90% accurate" became "about 80%" in its own FAQ.

8. **Never extend the Stoke brand to other products.** No Stoke gels, powders, meal kits or "Stoke Diet." One brand, one category, one word. Crest toothbrushes and Heinz ketchup chips are the cautionary tales: each borrowed a strong name and blurred it.
