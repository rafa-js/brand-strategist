# Stoke: Brand Strategy

## Brand Identity

| Element | Decision |
|---------|----------|
| **Brand Name** | Stoke |
| **Category** | Fuel tracker: a new branch diverging from the calorie tracker, judging food by the training ahead instead of by a daily budget. Per the Law of the Category, Stoke does not fight for a rung on the calorie ladder MyFitnessPal owns; it creates a ladder it can lead, as Red Bull created "energy drink" instead of fighting Coca-Cola for cola. |
| **Word to Own** | Fuel |
| **Verbal Nail** | "Fuel, not calories." |
| **Tagline** | Fuel, not calories. |

## The Core Idea

A locomotive's stoker doesn't shovel coal at a steady rate: they read the track ahead, feed the fire before the climb, and ease off on the flat. That is how people who train should eat, and it is what Stoke does: it looks at the session you have next and tells you whether what's on your plate will fuel it. The name, the metaphor and the position are one idea: to stoke is to feed the fire for the work ahead.

## Product

- **What it is:** a food tracker that scores what you eat by what it will do for your goal and your next session, and hands you one swap when it falls short.
- **How it works:** snap a plate (or scan a barcode, or say it). Stoke identifies the food, reads your training calendar (Strava, Garmin, Apple Health, or a plan you enter) and your goal (build muscle, run a marathon, race HYROX), and returns a Fuel Score with one line on why. The design leans on what photos read well (what the food is and when you eat it) rather than on gram-precise calorie totals, which photo apps undercount by about a third (NIH/NIDDK, 2026). Stoke asks a single portion question only when the answer would change the score.
- **The branded artifact: the Fuel Score (0-100)**, recalculated for each plate against the session ahead:

| Component | What It Measures | Why It Matters |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|
| **Goal fit** | Whether the plate's makeup matches the goal: protein-forward for building muscle, carbohydrate-forward for endurance volume, both for hybrid training | Muscle gains plateau around 1.6 g/kg/day of protein (Morton et al., 2018); endurance training of 1-3 h/day calls for 6-10 g/kg/day of carbohydrate (Thomas, Erdman and Burke, 2016) |
| **Timing** | Whether the food suits its place relative to the next session: fast, low-fibre carbohydrate before; carbohydrate plus protein after; fibre and micronutrients on easy days | The joint position stand advises avoiding "choices high in fat/protein/fibre" before events and periodizing to "the needs of daily training sessions" (Thomas et al., 2016) |
| **Dose** | Whether the amount is in range for this meal: about 0.25-0.4 g/kg of protein, and carbohydrate scaled to the session's length and intensity | ISSN: 0.25 g/kg or 20-40 g of protein per dose (Jäger et al., 2017); 0.4 g/kg per meal across four meals (Schoenfeld and Aragon, 2018) |
| **Quality** | Protein quality and leucine; fibre, iron and calcium when the day allows; processing judged by context, not by rule | 700-3,000 mg of leucine per dose (Jäger et al., 2017); "nutrient-rich carbohydrate sources" for daily fueling (Thomas et al., 2016) |

The weights differ by goal and are published. For muscle building, daily protein adequacy outweighs timing, because timing effects disappear once total protein is controlled (Schoenfeld, Aragon and Krieger, 2013); for endurance and HYROX, timing and session carbohydrate carry the most weight. The method is reviewed by sports dietitians before launch.

- **The Fuel Floor (safety by design):** energy is watched as a floor, never budgeted as a ceiling. When logged intake runs low against training load for several days, Stoke says so plainly and suggests adding fuel; it never praises eating less. This uses the sport's own risk model, energy availability (Mountjoy et al., 2023), with "at risk" language because self-reported intake inflates apparent under-fueling (McHaffie et al., 2025).
- **Convergence flag (Origin of Brands):** Stoke joins a tracker and a score, and the risk is being read as two categories glued together, "Cal AI plus Yuka," which converged products rarely survive. Mitigation: present one thing, the fuel tracker; lead every screen and message with the score in its session context; keep logging as invisible plumbing; never market the features as a bundle.
- **What Stoke leaves out (Law of Sacrifice):** no calorie budget and no "over" warnings; no weight goals and no cut mode; no meal plans; no generic health grade; no streaks or rewards for eating less. Each of these would serve a different word than fuel. In-N-Out's deliberately tiny menu is the model: subtract what the category treats as mandatory.

### The Signature Interaction / Element

**The One Swap**: every plate that falls short of the session ahead gets exactly one swap, the single change that most raises its Fuel Score. Illustratively: "Swap the granola for a white bagel with honey: 54 to 86 for tomorrow's 30 km." It sits under the score, on the night-before nudge and on the share card. One swap, never a plan, never a lecture: the position in miniature, and the opposite of the meal plans that elite fueling apps prescribe. Offering swaps improved shoppers' choices more than Nutri-Score labels alone in a randomized trial (Jansen et al., 2021), although swap evidence so far comes from the general population and shows modest effects.

### The User Journey

| Stage | Experience | What the User Feels/Does |
|-------|-----------|--------------------------|
| **1. Snap** | Week one: photograph meals as usual, pick a goal and connect training (or enter the plan). No grades on day one; Stoke learns the week. | "This is faster than my calorie app, and nothing is telling me off." |
| **2. Score** | Every plate gets a Fuel Score for the session ahead, with one line of why. | Notices that the "healthy" breakfast scores low before a long run; gets curious. |
| **3. Swap** | The One Swap appears before key sessions, plus a night-before nudge. | Takes one swap and feels the difference on a long run, heavy day or race simulation. |
| **4. Race week** | Stoke looks further ahead: carbohydrate loading, the race-morning plate, fuel per hour during the race, recovery. | Trusts Stoke with the day that matters, and shares the race-week card. |

The journey carries one abrupt conversion, flagged in the guardrails: people switching from calorie apps will look for the budget, and it is gone by design. Onboarding explains "a fuel floor, not a calorie ceiling," and energy figures stay available in a detail view for the curious, never as a target.

- Target user: people who train for something (a race time, a heavier lift, a HYROX finish) and measure progress there, not on the scale. Ambitious amateurs with day jobs who train like athletes but have been handed dieting tools or pro meal plans.

### Elevator Pitch

> "If you train for something, a marathon, a HYROX, a bigger squat, you've probably tried a calorie app. They're mostly built to help you lose weight. But the bagel a health app grades 'average' is exactly right the morning of a long run, and the sports-nutrition guidelines say so. Stoke scores what you actually eat against the session you've got next, and gives you one swap, not a meal plan. Snap your plate. Get fueled. Fuel, not calories."

### Competitive Repositioning

**Move 1: Demote the calorie to a weight tool (Law of the Opposite, delivered with the Law of Candor).** Concede what is true: digital self-monitoring was linked to weight loss in 74% of reported occurrences across 39 trials (Patel et al., 2021). Then reposition it: that is the dieter's job, while the athlete's guidelines time nutrition "in relation to sport rather than general daily targets" (Thomas et al., 2016). MyFitnessPal ("Calorie Counter") and Cal AI are named after the calorie; they cannot follow Stoke here without contradicting their own names. This is the Uncola move: 7-Up became the alternative to cola by defining itself against it.

**Move 2: Reclassify health scores as general-population tools (Law of Division).** Quote the incumbent against itself: Nutri-Score "cannot be used" for sport nutrition products because it was built for "the needs of the general population" (Santé publique France, 2025). Every food score the prospect already knows, from Yuka to ZOE to Apple's iOS 27, belongs to that general-population branch; Stoke's score belongs to training. Tylenol entered a mind full of aspirin the same way, "for the millions who should not take aspirin": health scores are for everyone's long-term health; Stoke is for the millions who train.

**Move 3: Demote the meal plan to a pro tool (Law of Sacrifice, and Zag's "the only, not the better").** "Plans assume a chef. Stoke works with the food you already eat." The weapons: Hexis's own partner review calls its logging "the grind" (Roadman Cycling, 2026); raising young athletes' nutrition knowledge did not change what they ate (Heikkilä et al., 2019); swaps outperformed labels in a randomized trial (Jansen et al., 2021). Google won search with a near-empty page while the portals piled on features; Stoke wins with one swap while the plans pile on meals.

## Naming

### Name: Stoke

- **Meaning 1**: To stoke a fire is to add fuel so it burns hotter: the product's job in one verb.
- **Meaning 2**: "Stoke up" means "to eat one's fill; to eat copious amounts" ("stoke up on pizza," Wiktionary): the name is, literally, eating for energy.
- **Meaning 3**: "Stoked" means thrilled and fired up: the feeling of a personal best.
- **Tone**: warm, physical, fired up, a little irreverent. Not clinical, not dieting, not gym-bro.
- **Verb test**: "Stoke your long run." "Did you stoke up before the race?" Natural, because "stoke up" already exists as a verb about eating.

### Naming Principles Applied

| Principle | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| No category words in the name | Clean: "stoke" is not food, fuel, track, cal or macro, and it stands apart from the many "Fuel" names in the niche (Fuelin, Exact Fuel, Fuel: AI Nutrition) |
| 1-2 syllables | One |
| Easy to spell and pronounce | One obvious spelling in English; reads cleanly in Spanish and German |
| Evokes the idea, not the function | Evokes feeding a fire for the work ahead, not logging food |
| Multiple reinforcing meanings | Three: fueling a fire, eating for energy, fired up |
| Built-in visual hammer potential | Yes, with a constraint: the drawable image is fire, and a flame commonly stands for "calories burned" in fitness apps, so the hammer is the ember: fuel glowing and ready, not burning |

**Caveat:** the name space is crowded and must be cleared before committing. Found on 5 October 2026: App Store apps named "Stoked Metabolic Coaching" (fitness and nutrition coaching), "STOKED Community" (a CrossFit and HYROX gym app), "The Stoke" (studio booking) and "ActiveStoke" (Stoke-on-Trent leisure centres); trainer Kira Stokes markets "The Stoked Method™"; "STOKE" is a pending US trademark filing by Stoke Space Technologies in software and scientific services (serial 98007395); and in the UK the word also means Stoke-on-Trent and Stoke City FC. The domains stoke.app, stokeapp.com, getstoke.com, stoke.fit, stoke.run, getstoke.app, trystoke.com, joinstoke.com, stoke.coach, stokeup.app and stokefuel.com are registered; stokeapp.co was not. Required before launch: trademark clearance in the US, EU and UK (classes 9, 42 and 44, plus 41 if coaching is ever offered), an App Store name check (for example "Stoke: Fuel Tracker"), and a domain plan. If clearance fails, keep the strategy and re-run naming against the same six principles.

## Strategic Guardrails

1. **Never lead with AI or photo logging.** Logging is the mechanism and table stakes: at least eight competitors launched photo logging between November 2024 and May 2026, and Apple gives a version away in iOS 27. Leading with it puts Stoke on Cal AI's ladder, which MyFitnessPal now owns.

2. **Never call Stoke a calorie counter, diet app or weight-loss tool, and never add weight targets or a cut mode.** A calorie budget makes Stoke a smaller MyFitnessPal; giving up weight goals is the sacrifice that makes "fuel" ownable. It also keeps Stoke away from the motive most associated with later disordered behavior among app users: weight management (Hahn et al., 2024). This is the one abrupt conversion in the user journey: switchers from calorie apps will ask for the budget back. Answer with onboarding that explains the fuel floor, never by quietly reintroducing a ceiling.

3. **Never praise eating less, and treat the Fuel Floor as launch-blocking.** Before launch, a sports dietitian and an eating-disorder specialist review every message about low intake; messaging uses "at risk" language and points to professional help. Athletes carry higher eating-disorder rates than non-athletes (13.5% vs. 4.6% among Norwegian elite athletes; Sundgot-Borgen and Torstveit, 2004).

4. **Never moralize food.** No "good," "bad," "clean," "junk" or "cheat." Every score names its context ("right for km 25 of tomorrow's long run"), because a gel scored without context reads as an endorsement of junk food, and ultra-processed foods carry real concerns outside sport (Forbes et al., 2026).

5. **Never claim more than the evidence supports.** No "proven to make you faster," no accuracy percentages, no "first." Say "built on the ACSM, ISSN and IOC guidance" and publish the Fuel Score method. The most quotable failure in this category is an accuracy claim: Cal AI's "90% accurate" became "about 80%" in its own FAQ.

6. **Keep citation discipline.** Every public number traces to the evidence brief, and joint position stands and independent trials beat industry-funded studies; several widely cited protein and fueling papers carry dairy, beef, supplement or gel-maker ties.

7. **Never invent HYROX science.** No published study measures how to fuel HYROX. Say that Stoke applies the endurance and strength guidelines to a race that is about half running, and fund or partner on real data rather than implying it exists.

8. **Never extend the Stoke brand to other products.** No Stoke gels, powders, meal kits or "Stoke Diet." One brand, one category, one word. Crest toothbrushes and Heinz ketchup chips are the cautionary tales: each borrowed a strong name and blurred it.
