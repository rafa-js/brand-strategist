# Stoke: PR Launch Narrative

## Core Story

> Fueling apps work. Their users say they take "the guesswork out" of what and when to eat, and the science is settled: athletes should eat to the training they have planned. The guidelines sports dietitians apply in pro sport come down to a handful of habits: carbohydrate that rises and falls with the training, protein at every meal, never starting a key session on empty. But the apps that teach them were built for going all in. One leading app offers no free trial because, in its own words, it "works best when you're all in." It charges $139 a year and features Ironman world champions. Meanwhile the big events keep setting records: HYROX counts more than 1.5 million participants last season, London drew 1.34 million ballot applications, and in one 2025 study only 2 of 38 marathoners hit the race carbohydrate target. Stoke copies the science and builds the habits into a real week: free to start, about half the price, synced to your plan, logged in seconds. Pro habits don't need a pro's life.

### Why This Story Is Newsworthy

| Criteria | How Stoke Delivers |
|----------|--------------------|
| **The hook** | "Pro habits don't need a pro's life." It challenges the premium fueling apps without attacking their science, and it tells millions of people training around full lives that pro fueling guidelines come down to a handful of habits they can keep. |
| **Candor** | Stoke says openly that it copied what works: the targets that adapt to training, grounded in the joint position stands. It publishes what it changed and why (the price, the trial, the cancellation policy, the target method and its limits), so journalists can quote a startup that credits its competitors and says what it cannot prove. |
| **Evidence-backed** | The ACSM/AND/DC joint position (2016), the ISSN protein stand (2017), the IOC consensus on REDs (2023), fueling and knowledge studies outside pro sport (Hansen et al., 2014; *Eur J Sport Sci*, 2025; Csanaky et al., 2025), competitors' published prices and policies (doc 1), and the participation data (HYROX, London Marathon Events, Strava). Every claim stays inside them: Stoke says what its targets are built on, never that it makes anyone faster. |
| **Timely** | HYROX's growth and Infront's September 2026 sale of its majority stake to an L Catterton-led consortium, London's record ballot (May 2026), Strava's purchase of Runna and its million clubs, Hexis's direct-to-consumer push (June 2026), MyFitnessPal's HYROX Tampa sponsorship (2026), and Apple's iOS 27 food ratings (September 2026). |
| **Universal** | Everyone training for something big has been told to "fuel properly," then met a paywall, an app built for athletes with a coach, or a calorie app that told them to eat less the night before a long run. |

## The Narrative Arc (Three Acts)

**Act 1: Fueling works, and it's a handful of habits (concede the status quo)**
"The science is settled, and the best fueling apps follow it: eat to the training you have planned. More carbohydrate on hard days, protein at every meal, fuel before, during and after the sessions that matter. In pro sport, dietitians make it habit. The apps' users say they take the guesswork out. Nobody needs to reinvent that."

**Act 2: Built for going all in (reveal the gap with evidence)**
"But look at who they're built for. No free trial, because the app 'works best when you're all in.' $139 a year. Ironman champions on the homepage, and coaching that costs up to $1,500 a month. And by its own count, a little over 3,000 age-group athletes a month were using it. Meanwhile the big events keep setting records: HYROX counts more than 1.5 million participants last season, and 1.34 million people applied to run London. In one study, only 2 of 38 marathoners hit the race carbohydrate target."

**Act 3: Pro habits, real life (deliver Stoke)**
"Stoke copies the science and builds the pros' habits into a real week. Free to start, about half the price, synced to the plan you already follow, logging that takes seconds, and a night-before check that tells you whether you're fueled for tomorrow. It spreads through creators and coaches who train around full lives, not through champions. Fueling shouldn't ask you to go all in. Stoke builds pro habits into the life you already have."

Same story at every scale: press pitch, podcast interview, 60-second social video.

## Shareability Check (STEPPS)

| STEPPS Lever | How Stoke's Story Delivers |
|--------------|----------------------------|
| **Social Currency** | Knowing the few habits that matter (carbohydrate per hour, protein spread across the day) makes you the person in the run club who fuels like they mean it, without obsessing. Club codes and early access through a coach are an insider's ticket, and the weekly card ("4 of 5 key sessions fueled") says "I prepare," with no badge or streak. |
| **Triggers** | The night before a long run or heavy session, when the nudge arrives; the work lunch, the meal no plan controls; race week; Sunday shopping; and every time another app asks for a card before showing anything. |
| **Emotion** | High-arousal indignation at being priced out and paywalled ("it wanted my data and my card before it showed me anything"), plus the excitement of finding that pro fueling guidelines come down to a handful of habits anyone can keep, and the surprise that it mostly means eating more carbohydrate before the sessions that matter. Never guilt, which is low-arousal and off-brand. |
| **Public** | The ember on shared race-week and weekly cards, club fueling weeks, and fueling notes in Strava activity descriptions: visible, persistent residue after the launch. |
| **Practical Value** | One-pagers that travel without the app: "The five pro habits" (carbs follow the training, protein at every meal, never start a key session under-fueled, fuel the long ones, rehearse race day); "The Bagel Test" (one bagel, three goals, three ways to top it); "Race carbohydrate per hour" (30-60 g/h for 1-2.5 hours, up to 90 g/h beyond 2.5-3 hours); "Protein per meal" (about 0.25-0.4 g/kg). |
| **Stories** | The archetype: the athlete who thought fueling was for pros, learned that it comes down to a few habits (carbohydrate before the hard sessions, protein at every meal) and built them around a desk job, meal by meal. The app that fits a real week is the turning point; remove it and the story ends at the paywall. |

**Trojan horse test:** retold without naming Stoke, the story becomes "fueling apps work but were built for going all in; pro fueling guidelines come down to a handful of habits, and anyone can keep them with targets that are free to start and synced to their plan." The claim survives, and its ending points at one thing: a fueling app that builds pro habits into a real week, the branch Stoke names and the only product built to that brief. If a competitor adds a free trial, the story still holds, because the rest of the build (the price, the plan sources, the logging, the channels) does not change with one setting. Like Blendtec's "Will It Blend?", where the story cannot end without the blender, the story cannot end without the app that fits a real week. **Pass.**

## Influencer Strategy

### Who to Target

| Target | Why | How |
|--------|-----|-----|
| **Club and gym coaches** (HYROX affiliate gyms, run clubs, strength gyms) | They talk to athletes every week, and the channel is the opposite of Fuelin's pros: HYROX counts about 15,000 affiliate gyms, and new clubs on Strava nearly quadrupled in 2025, to 1 million in total. | A free coach view of their athletes' fueling consistency (with each athlete's consent), club codes that give members three months of Premium, and a club fueling-night kit. |
| **Creators who train around full lives** (marathon, hybrid, lifting) | The faces of the brand: credible because they live the real-life half, and their followers train around work too. They keep pro habits in a real week, filmed to a pro standard. | Access plus the evidence one-pager, and the single request below; their real targets and night-before checks become public artifacts. |
| **Sports dietitians (RD, CSSD) active on social** | The authority behind the habits, never the face of the ads: their endorsement transfers clinical authority, and their review protects the Energy Floor. | Advisory roles, method review, co-authoring the evidence brief; paid for advisory work, never for posts. Their names on the method are the insider credential. |
| **Training-plan apps** (Runna and HYROX's online training partners) | Their users already plan their sessions; Stoke turns those plans into targets. | Integrations and co-marketing, pending feasibility and partner terms. |

### Who to Avoid

| Avoid | Why |
|-------|-----|
| **Pro athletes as the face** | Pro is the standard, not the spokesperson. Pro endorsements are Fuelin's and Hexis's channel, and a champion's face pictures the pros' life when Stoke promises the user's own. |
| **Weight-loss and "what I eat in a day to get lean" creators** | They tie Stoke to the diet paradigm, and their audiences track for the weight motive most linked to harm. |
| **Clean-eating and anti-ultra-processed purists** | They will attack context-based gel and bagel advice; pairing with them invites a "Stoke endorses junk food" story. |
| **Low-carb, keto and carnivore advocates** | They reject carbohydrate for training outright, and the ISSN found ketogenic diets largely neutral or detrimental for performance (Leaf et al., 2024). |
| **Supplement hype accounts** | They undermine credibility, and Stoke sells no supplements; it must not look like a funnel for them. |
| **Paid ambassadors of MyFitnessPal's or Myprotein's HYROX programs** | Conflicted promoters; their posts would read as paid switching. |

### The Influencer Brief

- They receive three months of access, the evidence one-pager ("The Pro Habit Gap"), the five pro habits one-pager and the target method.
- The narrative comes as context, not script: why Stoke exists (pro habits, built into a real week) and what it will not claim.
- One request: "Fuel one training block with Stoke, eating the way you normally do. When a target or the night-before check changes what you eat, tell people which pro habit it put into practice and how it fit your week."
- Disclosure is required: say that Stoke gave you access (FTC Endorsement Guides in the US, the CAP Code in the UK), and don't say it made you faster.
- No talking points. No hashtag requirements. The authentic content moment is the night before a hard session and race week.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why It Fails |
|---------|-------------|
| **Turning pro habits into pro identity (the single most dangerous)** | Pro ambassadors, "elite" language, "fuel like a pro," an "all in" commitment or a coaching upsell turn the habits into the pros' lifestyle, erase the real-life half of the position and leave a cheaper Fuelin with no reason to exist once Fuelin cuts its price. The opposite drift is as fatal: calling customers amateurs, beginners or weekend warriors tells them they are not serious, and nobody trains for something big to hear that. In press, never name Fuelin first; if a journalist does, use the locked line verbatim, "Fuelin works best when you're all in. Stoke builds pro habits into the life you already have.", then concede its strengths. |
| **Leading with the wrong element: price, AI logging or a feature** | "The cheapest fueling app" invites a price war Stoke can't win alone and frames it as a discount; "snap to log" puts it on Cal AI's ladder; leading with any feature invites a feature race and turns a reason to believe into the message. Lead with pro habits for real life; price, logging and features are reasons to believe. |
| **Advertising before the claim is established** | "Pro habits, real life" needs third parties (coaches, dietitians, journalists) to make it credible, and MyFitnessPal can outspend any startup. Red Bull and Starbucks were built by publicity first and advertising later. |
| **Copying the complaint generators** | Paywalls before value and cancellation mazes are the patterns behind most of Fuelin's low-rated reviews, and a few reviewers also resent its ads and marketing email (doc 1). In a word-of-mouth channel, each one turns a club member into a detractor. |
| **Weight-loss framing or gamified restriction** | Under-fueling risk is common in recreational athletes, and athletes carry elevated eating-disorder rates. Body-composition goals stay a setting behind the Energy Floor, never a message, and the safety review of every deficit setting and game mechanic is a launch requirement, not a patch. |
| **The overclaim** | "Proven to make you faster," "the most accurate," "the first," "syncs with Runna" before it does, "eat like a pro, race like one," "a habit in 21 days," a price comparison that is no longer true, or invented HYROX science: one dietitian's quote ends the credibility position, as Cal AI's slide from "90% accurate" to "about 80%" shows. |
| **Ambushing HYROX or its sponsors** | Unofficial event marketing using HYROX marks invites legal and community backlash, and MyFitnessPal and Myprotein hold the official slots. Go through coaches, gyms and research instead. |
