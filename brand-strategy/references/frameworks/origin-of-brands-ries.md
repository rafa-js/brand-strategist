# The Origin of Brands
### Al Ries & Laura Ries (2004)

> *"The way to build a powerful brand is not to converge with others, but to diverge — to create a new category."*

---

## Core Premise

Drawing on Darwin's theory of evolution, Ries & Ries argue that **brands and categories evolve the same way species do: through divergence, not convergence**. The technology industry's obsession with converged devices (the Swiss Army Knife strategy) consistently fails, while the brands that win are those that split off a new category and own it exclusively. The book is fundamentally a guide to **category creation as the highest form of brand strategy**.

---

## Key Highlights

### 1. The Divergence Principle
- Nature creates new species not by combining existing ones, but by splitting off a variation that thrives in a specific niche.
- Brands do the same: new categories are born when a variant of an existing product diverges enough to stand alone.
- **Example**: The PC diverged from the mainframe. The laptop diverged from the PC. The tablet diverged from the laptop. Each created a new category — and a new brand opportunity.

### 2. Why Convergence Fails
- Convergence is the instinct to combine two things: phone + camera, TV + computer, car + office.
- These combinations appeal to engineers and executives, but they rarely appeal to consumers, who prefer purpose-built tools.
- The converged product is usually worse at both functions than the dedicated alternatives.
- **Pattern**: Every major "convergence" product announcement generates hype, followed by market rejection or niche adoption at best.

### 3. The Opportunity in Divergence
- Every time a market leader gets large and multi-purpose, a divergence window opens.
- The winning move is to **take one attribute of the current category, amplify it, and build an entirely new brand around that single attribute**.
- You don't need to invent new technology — you need to name and own the diverged category before anyone else.

### 4. The Category Is the Strategy
- The most important brand decision is **what category your brand will lead**, not what your product's features are.
- Define the category narrowly enough that you can credibly claim to be #1.
- Brand names should reflect category ownership, not generic descriptions.

### 5. The Naming Imperative
- A diverged category needs a **new name** to succeed. Borrowing the parent category's brand name limits growth and confuses the position.
- New category + new name = the ability to own both the category ladder rung #1 *and* the word in the mind simultaneously.
- **Example**: Red Bull didn't call itself "Coke Energy." It named itself and its category simultaneously — "energy drink" was a category that barely existed before Red Bull defined it.

### 6. The Brand Divergence Matrix

| Situation | Recommended Move |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Large parent category with no clear sub-segment | Diverge into the sub-segment with a new brand |
| Existing product trying to converge with another | Stop — reposition as a specialist, not a generalist |
| Two brands competing in the same category | One should diverge to own a subcategory |
| New technology entering market | Name the category before competitors do |

### 7. The Tree Metaphor
- The authors use a branching tree to show how categories proliferate: beer → light beer → ultra-light, imported, craft, non-alcoholic.
- Each branch is a branding opportunity. The earlier you plant a flag, the more defensible the position.
- Companies should **map their category tree** to anticipate where divergence is imminent.

### 8. Internet & Digital Application
- The internet itself is a divergence story: e-commerce diverged from retail, search diverged from portals, social networks diverged from email.
- The brands that tried to be "everything online" (portals like AOL, Yahoo) lost to focused divergers (Google for search, Amazon for shopping, Facebook for social).
- The principle applies to every new digital vertical: own one diverged category rather than attempting to dominate many.

---

## Framework: Spotting a Divergence Opportunity

Ask these questions in sequence:

1. **What is the dominant category in this market?** (e.g., "smartphones")
2. **What unmet need exists within that category that no brand fully serves?** (e.g., "photography-first phones")
3. **Could that unmet need sustain an entirely separate category?** (e.g., "camera phones → pro-camera phones")
4. **What is the right name for this new category?** (Not a product name — a *category* name)
5. **Can you be first to claim this name publicly?** (PR, naming, positioning, before competitors react)

---

## Contrast with Convergence Thinking

| Convergence Thinking | Divergence Thinking |
|---------------------|-------------------|
| "Let's add features to serve more users" | "Let's strip away features to serve one user perfectly" |
| "One product for every need" | "One product, one need, one word in the mind" |
| "Bigger platform, more categories" | "Smaller category, total dominance" |
| Leads to mediocre multi-purpose products | Leads to category-defining brands |

---

## One-Line Summary
> Don't converge with your competitors. Diverge from them — and name the new category before they do.
