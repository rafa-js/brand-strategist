# The Frameworks

Compact working reference for the nine frameworks the templates cite: five Ries/Trout playbooks, Jonah Berger's Contagious for word-of-mouth design, and Marty Neumeier's three brand-design books (Zag, The Brand Gap, Scramble) for radical differentiation and agile strategy. Use it to name the right law in recommendations and to run the audit's 22-Laws sweep.

Each framework also has a full reference in this directory — with extended brand examples, step-by-step application frameworks, and common-mistakes lists. Read the full reference when a deliverable leans heavily on that framework:

## Positioning (Ries & Trout, 1981)

The battle for your brand happens in the prospect's mind, not in the market. Core concepts:

- **The mind is the battleground.** Perception beats product. You don't change minds — you work with what's already there.
- **Own a word.** The strongest position is a single word or concept the brand owns alone (Volvo = safety, Google = search). Two words is a weak position; three is none.
- **The ladder.** For every category, prospects hold a mental ladder of brands. Strategy depends on your rung: leaders defend, #2 attacks or takes the opposite, lower rungs find an open ladder (a new category).
- **The opposite test.** A real position has a viable opposite that a competitor could hold. If "for people who want quality" has no viable opposite, it isn't a position.
- **Repositioning.** To move into a mind that's full, hang a new idea on the competitor first ("Tylenol: for the millions who should not take aspirin").
- **Cherchez le créneau** — look for the hole: the open position (size, price, sex, age, time of day, distribution) nobody occupies.
- **Line-extension trap and the name.** The name is the hook for the position; stretching it across products blurs what it means.

## The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing (Ries & Trout, 1993)

1. **Leadership** — better to be first than better.
2. **Category** — if you can't be first in a category, create a new category you can be first in.
3. **Mind** — better to be first in the mind than first in the market.
4. **Perception** — marketing is a battle of perceptions, not products.
5. **Focus** — own a word in the prospect's mind.
6. **Exclusivity** — two companies cannot own the same word.
7. **Ladder** — strategy depends on which rung you occupy.
8. **Duality** — in the long run, every market becomes a two-horse race.
9. **Opposite** — if you're shooting for #2, your strategy is determined by the leader; be its opposite, not its imitation.
10. **Division** — over time, a category divides into two or more categories.
11. **Perspective** — marketing effects play out over the long term; short-term fixes (discounts, extensions) often reverse long-term.
12. **Line Extension** — the most violated law: stretching a brand's name onto new products dilutes it. Default to a new brand.
13. **Sacrifice** — to get something, give something up: product line, target market, or constant change.
14. **Attributes** — for every attribute, there is an opposite, effective attribute to own.
15. **Candor** — admit a negative and the prospect will give you a positive (Avis: "We're #2. We try harder." Listerine: "the taste you hate twice a day").
16. **Singularity** — in each situation, only one move produces substantial results; find the bold stroke.
17. **Unpredictability** — you can't predict the future; build flexibility, don't plan on competitors' plans.
18. **Success** — success leads to arrogance, and arrogance to failure (usually via line extension).
19. **Failure** — expect and accept failure; cut losses early.
20. **Hype** — the situation is often the opposite of how it appears in the press; real revolutions arrive unannounced.
21. **Acceleration** — build on trends, not fads.
22. **Resources** — without adequate funding, an idea won't get off the ground.

Common audit culprits to check first: Leadership, Category, Focus, Sacrifice, Line Extension, Perception, Mind.

## The Origin of Brands (Al & Laura Ries, 2004)

Categories behave like species: they **diverge**, they don't converge.

- **Divergence creates opportunity.** New categories split off from existing ones (computer → desktop → laptop → server...). The winning strategy is to spot a divergence early and claim the new branch with a **new brand name**.
- **Convergence fails.** Combination products and merged categories (the "everything device" pitched every decade) consistently lose to dedicated branches. Flag every convergence strategy with this risk.
- **First mover with a new name wins the branch.** Extending an existing name onto a new branch hands the category to whoever names it natively.
- **Naming the category** is as important as naming the brand: a crisp category name ("energy drink," "ride sharing") gives the mind a place to file the brand — at the top.

## The Fall of Advertising and the Rise of PR (Al & Laura Ries, 2002)

- **Advertising has no credibility; PR does.** Prospects discount what a brand says about itself and trust what third parties say about it.
- **Brands are built by PR, defended by advertising.** Launch with earned media — press, experts, word of mouth — until the claim is established; only then use advertising to repeat what the media already said.
- **Sequence is the strategy:** science/evidence first, seeding credible voices, pitching story-led media, launch, amplification — paid media last. The more radical the claim, the higher the credibility threshold before any ad spend.
- **A new category is a story; a better product is an ad.** Journalists cover what's first, not what's better — another reason category creation beats incremental claims.

## Contagious: Why Things Catch On (Jonah Berger, 2013)

Six levers (STEPPS) for engineering word of mouth into the PR narrative. Shareability serves the position; it makes the locked story travel, it never picks the story.

- **Social Currency:** people share what makes them look like insiders. Find the inner remarkability; mint secrets and exclusivity (Please Don't Tell).
- **Triggers:** top of mind means tip of tongue. Tie the story to a frequent cue in the prospect's week (Kit Kat and coffee breaks).
- **Emotion:** high-arousal feelings travel (awe, anger, anxiety, amusement); low-arousal sadness and contentment do not. When we care, we share.
- **Public:** observable behavior gets imitated. Design visible participation and behavioral residue (Movember mustaches, Apple's flipped logo).
- **Practical Value:** people pass along genuinely useful material. Package news you can use.
- **Stories:** ideas travel inside narratives. Run the **Trojan horse test**: if the story can be retold without the brand's idea, the virality is wasted (Blendtec's "Will It Blend" passes; Evian's roller babies failed).

## Visual Hammer (Laura Ries, 2012)

- **The nail and the hammer.** The verbal position is the nail; the visual is the hammer that drives it into the mind. Words alone fade; a visual locked to an idea endures (Marlboro's cowboy, Coke's contour bottle, the Aflac duck).
- **The hammer must encode the nail.** A beautiful visual that doesn't express the position is decoration. Evaluate every visual against the stated verbal position — never in isolation.
- **One color.** Pick the color the category leader doesn't own and use it with ruthless consistency. Multicolor works only when variety/completeness IS the position (NBC's peacock, Google).
- **Hammer candidates:** a shape, a color, a product form, a package, a founder, a symbol, an action. Test: distinctive in the category, meaningful to the position, simple enough to work at 16 pixels.
- **Consistency over novelty.** Hammers gain force through repetition over years; redesigns that chase fashion throw the accumulated equity away.

## Zag (Marty Neumeier, 2007)

Radical differentiation: when everybody zigs, zag. The operational sharpener for Positioning and Origin of Brands: it hands you the instrument to find and build the word the brand owns.

- **Be the only, not the better.** "Better" invites comparison with the incumbent; "only" ends it. In a cluttered market, small differences read as no difference.
- **The onliness statement.** "Our \_\_\_ is the only \_\_\_ that \_\_\_." If you can't complete it truthfully, you don't have a zag. Sharpen it with WHAT (category), HOW (point of difference), WHO (audience), WHERE (market), WHY (need state), WHEN (trend). This is a stricter, more buildable form of the positioning statement.
- **Focus by subtraction.** A zag requires sacrifice; often the zag is removing what the category treats as mandatory (Google's near-empty page, In-N-Out's tiny menu, Cirque du Soleil dropping the animals).
- **White space, enemy, tribe, wave.** Find the empty spot on the brandscape; name an enemy to sharpen contrast; design for the tribe that will love you most; ride a real rising trend, not a fad.
- **Extend with new zags, not line extension.** Grow a portfolio of focused brands; never stretch one name until it owns nothing (echoes the Law of Line Extension and divergence).

## The Brand Gap (Marty Neumeier, 2003)

A brand is a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or organization: not what you say it is, what they say it is. The bridge between business strategy (logic, left brain) and design (magic, right brain); brands die in the gap between the two.

- **Charisma is the goal.** A charismatic brand is one for which people believe there's no substitute (Apple, Harley-Davidson, IKEA). It is the escape from the commodity price war, and any brand can earn it.
- **Five disciplines.** **Differentiate** (Who are you? What do you do? Why does it matter?), **Collaborate** (orchestrate the network around one vision via a brand steward), **Innovate** (design to look like nobody else; name well), **Validate** (test light with real people), **Cultivate** (the brand as a living system, true to itself over time).
- **The swap test.** If you can swap your name, logo, color, or tagline with a competitor's and it still works, it isn't distinctive enough. (Reinforces the opposite test and hammer distinctiveness.)
- **Aim for identification.** Differentiation evolved from features to benefits to experience to identification ("this brand is me"). Identification is the durable end of the ladder.

## Scramble (Marty Neumeier, 2018)

Agile strategy: run strategy like a design process, in fast loops, in weeks not quarters. Governs *how* the work is done, not which position is right — the working method layered on top of the position theory.

- **5 Qs × 5 Ps = agile strategy.** Apply the five Ps of design thinking to the five Qs of strategy, worked in parallel, to collapse 6–18 months into under six weeks.
- **The five Qs:** Purpose (why we exist), Customer (who we serve), Category (where we compete), Positioning (how we win, answered with the onliness statement), Culture (how we grow).
- **The five Ps:** Problemizing (frame the real problem behind the problem), Pinballing (generate many options), Probing (examine from multiple angles), Prototyping (build a rough model to test fast), Proofing (test with the market; keep what survives).
- **Abductive reasoning.** Strategy is the logic of "what could be," not just "what is." Design the future from first principles; build to think; reframe before you solve.
- **Use it when** the work has to move fast or has stalled: treat the five-document set as prototypes to iterate, not a waterfall, and reframe the brief before prescribing.
